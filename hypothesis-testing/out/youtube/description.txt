Your friend's coin decides who pays for coffee, and in a month it lands heads 15 times out of 20. Is it rigged, or just luck? If the coin were fair, only about 21 of 1,000 people would get 15 or more heads: a p value of about 0.02. A 15-minute explainer for people who don't think of themselves as "math people".

📐 KEY FORMULAS
• p = P(data at least this extreme | H₀)
  p = the p value · P = probability · | = given, assuming · H₀ = the null hypothesis (the boring explanation: the coin is fair)
• Reject H₀ when p < α
  α (alpha) = the significance level: a cut-off chosen BEFORE you look, often 0.05 (1 in 20)

✅ WORKED EXAMPLE
• 20 flips of a fair coin can land 1,048,576 ways; 21,700 of them have 15+ heads → p = 21,700 ÷ 1,048,576 ≈ 0.021
• Two-sided (rigged either way): p ≈ 0.041 · 14 heads: p ≈ 0.058 (not significant at 0.05)
• False alarms with 'rigged at 15+': ≈ 2.1% · a coin that lands heads 70% of the time is caught ≈ 42%, missed ≈ 58%
• 100 flips, cut-off 59: false alarms ≈ 4.4%, the 70% coin caught ≈ 99%
• A 50.2% coin over a million flips: 502,000 heads, p ≈ 0.00003, yet only ≈ 2 extra coffees per 1,000
• If 1 coin in 100 is rigged, a coin with 15+ heads is still fair ≈ 83% of the time (rigged ≈ 17%)
• 20 tests of nothing at 0.05: ≈ 64% chance at least one looks significant

🧭 THE RECIPE
1. State the null: the boring explanation
2. Choose α first, before you look (often 0.05)
3. Picture chance: the distribution if H₀ is true
4. Find the tail: the share at least as extreme as your result
5. Decide, then size it: p vs α, and always report the effect size

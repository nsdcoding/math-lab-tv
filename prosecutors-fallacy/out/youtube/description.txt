The prosecutor's fallacy explained visually, for Machine Learning (ML), Data Science (DS), Finance and Computer Science (CS).
See why P(evidence | innocent) is not P(innocent | evidence), how to count the crowd, and why the base rate is the missing piece.

In a city of 8 million people, Sam's DNA matches a partial trace from a crime scene. An expert says an innocent person would match only 1 time in a million, and the prosecutor says that means a 1 in a million chance Sam is innocent. Count the crowd, and about 8 innocent people match too: Sam is innocent about 8 times in 9. A 15-minute explainer for people who don't think of themselves as "math people".

🌍 WHERE YOU'LL USE IT
• Machine Learning / ML (9:55): a face-matching camera with 1 false match in 10,000, scanning 50,000 fans: about 5 false alerts + 1 real, so an alert is right only about 1 time in 6.
• Data Science / DS (10:18): a p value is P(results this extreme | nothing going on), not P(nothing going on | results). Same coin, p ≈ 0.021, can still be fair ≈ 83% of the time.
• Finance (10:37): a fraud model that wrongly flags 1 honest payment in 1,000: per million honest payments and 100 frauds, 90 ÷ 1,090 ≈ 1 flag in 12 is real fraud.
• Computer Science / CS (11:00): search 5 million DNA profiles for a 1 in a million match and expect about 5 innocent hits, even if the culprit isn't in the database.

📐 KEY FORMULAS
• P(A | B) = the chance of A, given B (the bar | means 'given': we already know B)
• P(match | innocent) = 1 in 1,000,000 is NOT P(innocent | match)
• P(guilty | match) = guilty people who match ÷ everyone who matches
  = 1 ÷ (1 + 8) = 1/9 in the city example (Bayes' theorem, done by counting)

✅ WORKED EXAMPLE
• City of 8,000,000: 7,999,999 innocent × 1 in a million ≈ 8 innocent matches, + 1 culprit = 9
• P(guilty | match) ≈ 1 in 9 · P(innocent | match) ≈ 8 in 9 ≈ 89% (about 889,000× bigger than 1 in a million)
• Same trace, different crowd: village of 1,000 → ≈ 999 in 1,000 guilty · country of 60 million → ≈ 1 in 61
• Before the match Sam was 1 in 8,000,000; after it, 1 in 9: strong evidence, not proof
• Medical test (from the Bayes video): P(positive | healthy) = 1 in 10, but P(healthy | positive) = 99 ÷ 108 = 11 in 12
• Real case: 1 in 8,543 squared ≈ 1 in 73 million (two errors: treating the deaths as unrelated, and the swap)

🧭 THE RECIPE
1. Find the given: what does the number assume is true?
2. Flip it: is that the question you actually care about?
3. Find the crowd: the base rate, how many could match?
4. Count the matches: crowd × false-match rate, + the real ones
5. Divide: real matches ÷ all matches, then combine any other evidence honestly
